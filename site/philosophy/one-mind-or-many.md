## What this is

I took these notes on November 4 and 5 while reading Sapiens by Yuval Noah Harari. They are my understanding of his points, written from memory of what I read. I have not checked them against the book page by page. Treat the claims as his as I understood them, and the questions as mine.

## A question he does not answer

As I noted it, Yuval asks a question and leaves it open. Cunning and manipulation helped rulers hold kingdoms for longer. Women are often good at both. So why do women not run the world? He does not give a settled answer. I find that honest. I do not have one either, and I will not invent one here.

## Cultures change without anyone steering them

My first note: cultures evolve with no outside push. Nobody sat down and chose the shape of a culture. It shifts through small acts by many people, and the result is not planned.

## Ideals contradict each other

My second note: a culture holds ideals that cannot all be satisfied at once. Liberty and equality are the example I wrote down. More liberty lets some people pull ahead. More equality limits what people may do. A culture that claims both is carrying a contradiction.

## Tension is where the energy comes from

As I understood it, he treats the contradictions as a feature. Conflict and unsolved dilemmas push people to think, argue and try new things. I wrote this in my own words: tension breeds creativity and dynamism, and consistency is the playground of the dull.

That matches something I see in code. A system with one rule and no exceptions is easy to reason about, and it also never has to grow. Real systems carry competing demands, such as speed against safety. The arguments over them are where the design work happens. I do not claim a culture is a codebase. It is a comparison from my own field.

## Living with it: cognitive dissonance

People hold beliefs that clash and carry on. Psychologists call the discomfort cognitive dissonance. One way to read the idea is that we feel it, work around it, and sometimes change a belief. That reading is mine. A culture may do the same at a larger scale. I do not know how well the comparison holds.

## The question I cannot close

My last note is the one I keep returning to. From a wide view, history looks like a move toward one mind. Small cultures merge into larger ones. The larger ones become mega cultures. Then they split again, as Christianity did into denominations. So the pull toward one mind seems to come with a pull away from it.

Two questions follow.

- Do we want to become one mind?
- If we did, would it destroy individualism?

If tension is what keeps a culture alive, then full agreement would remove the thing that powers it. That is my guess, not a finding. It could be wrong. A world with shared values might still hold plenty of disagreement about everything else.

## What I might be wrong about

- I wrote the points from memory. Harari may frame them differently, and he may hedge where I did not.
- Liberty against equality is one example. I have not checked whether he uses it that way.
- The splits in religion may have causes other than a need for tension. I have not studied them.
- "Consistency is the playground of the dull" is my line. It sounds good, and good-sounding lines are easy to believe. I have not tested it.

## What I took from it

1. Cultures contradict themselves and still last. The contradictions may be part of why.
2. Liberty and equality cannot both be maximised. A culture lives by balancing them.
3. Unsolved tension pushes people to create. Perfect consistency removes the push.
4. History shows merging and splitting together. I do not know which wins.
5. Open question for me: do I want one mind, or many?
