# 2026-09-16

## What happened

Miguel described a gap: he can gather technical knowledge quickly and reproduce it, but
struggles to explain the underlying mechanism, reasoning, or tradeoffs when asked directly.
This shows up in interviews. He suspects it bleeds into other parts of his life too.

## Separating the categories

- What he knows: he can implement and reproduce technical solutions.
- What he's assuming: that this means he doesn't "actually understand" the material, and that
  the cause is laziness.
- What remains unknown: which of the following actually describes what's happening,
  procedural only understanding, weak retrieval, disconnected pieces never assembled into a
  model, learning by imitation rather than reconstruction, or limited practice explaining
  things out loud. Possibly some combination.

## Investigation opened

A concrete test was proposed: pick something he believes he knows well (Redis caching,
database indexes, HTTP, Spring dependency injection), and without looking anything up,
answer what it is, why it exists, how it works, what would happen if it were removed. Then
examine exactly where the explanation breaks. This gives evidence instead of a label.

## Investigation, continued

Ran the retrieval test on database indexing. The explanation came out complete and correctly
ordered: problem, mechanism, tradeoff. No gaps, no missing reasoning.

Immediately after finishing it, predicted losing the audience. That prediction wasn't
supported by anything in the explanation itself. It came after a correct answer, not during
a struggling one.

A distinction raised independently, drawn from Ibn Arabi, gave a better name for what's
actually being asked: not "do I know this well enough" but "can I read the situation and
choose the right register for it." Different skill. The test result says the first isn't the
problem here. The second remains untested.

## Learn

The original hypothesis, a retrieval gap, doesn't hold, at least not for this topic. What's
better supported: the explanation was fine, and the self-prediction of failure wasn't tied to
the explanation's actual quality. Something closer to anticipating rejection, or not trusting
a finished answer to be enough, looks more relevant now.

## Govern

One thing to try, not a rule yet: give the one-sentence version first, then stop and let the
question decide what comes next, instead of pre-emptively going deep to avoid seeming thin.

## Carrying forward

- What happened: tested the hypothesis once, against real material.
- What I learned: the gap isn't retrieval. It's closer to trusting a complete answer to be
  complete, and choosing depth by the situation rather than by default.
- What remains uncertain: whether this holds outside indexing, and whether the
  self-prediction pattern shows up outside technical explanations too.
- What I want to do next: try the one-sentence-then-pause approach once, somewhere low
  stakes, and see what actually happens versus what's predicted to happen.
